# Jeff Bezos: Work backwards from the customer; bet long on what won't change

## In one minute
- Left a Wall Street job in 1994 after seeing web usage growing 2,300% a year ("the less safe path") [13]; started with books, built Amazon into retail, Marketplace, Prime, AWS and Kindle; founded Blue Origin (2000) and bought The Washington Post personally (2013) [12][24][28].
- Habit 1: start from the customer and write the launch announcement before building ("working backwards"), rather than asking "what else can our skills do?" [4][17].
- Habit 2: sort decisions into one-way doors (slow, careful) and two-way doors (fast, at ~70% of the information), then measure only the inputs you control and stay patient for years [5][7][8].
- He wrote failure into the plan: Auctions and zShops flopped before Marketplace, and the Fire Phone flopped before Echo/Alexa. He argued failures should get bigger as the company grows [6][10].

## Mental models (what they believe)

### Work backwards from the customer, not forward from your skills
- **Belief:** Building from what you're already good at ("skills-forward") means the company "will never be driven to develop fresh skills". So start from the customer need and acquire the skills it demands [4].
- **Real decision:** Kindle (vision from about 2004): Amazon "had never designed or built a hardware device". Instead of changing the vision to fit its skills, it hired hardware talent. Kindle launched in November 2007 and sold out in 5.5 hours [4][25]. Since 2004, most major Amazon launches began as a PR/FAQ: a press release under one page plus up to five pages of FAQs, often rewritten ten or more times. Most were never built, which the authors call "a feature, not a bug" [17].
- **Ask yourself:** What will a customer say on launch day, and would anyone care?
- **In a founder's prompts:** With it: "Draft the one-page announcement a customer would read for offline mode, then the FAQ, then the plan." Without it: "We already have a websocket service. What else can we build on it?"

### Name the door before deciding: one-way vs two-way
- **Belief:** A few decisions are "consequential and irreversible". Most "are changeable, reversible – they're two-way doors". Big companies wrongly run the slow, heavyweight process on everything [7][16].
- **Real decision:** In the 2016 letter he says most decisions should be made with about 70% of the information you wish you had. He greenlit an Amazon Studios original he doubted, writing "I disagree and commit" [8]. He challenged staff in December 2004 to speed up free shipping, and Prime launched two months later [19].
- **Ask yourself:** If this turns out wrong, can I undo it in a week?
- **In a founder's prompts:** With it: "This is reversible. Pick the simplest option, put it behind a flag, ship today." / "Ledger schema change is a one-way door. Write the migration and rollback plan first." Without it: three rounds of option comparison for a button layout, then a casual "just change the settlement table".

### Build strategy on what will not change in ten years
- **Belief:** The more important question is "what's not going to change in the next 10 years". Effort spent there keeps paying back [14].
- **Real decision:** He bet the retail business on low price and fast delivery: "It is difficult for us to imagine that ten years from now, customers will want higher prices" [4]. At AWS: "It's impossible to imagine that people would say: I love AWS, but I wish it were a little less reliable" [14]. Prime turned fast delivery into a subscription in 2005 and passed 100 million paid members by 2018 [9][19].
- **Ask yourself:** What will my customer still want in 2036?
- **In a founder's prompts:** With it: "Make the product survive a network drop at peak load. That need never changes." Without it: a new trend-driven feature every week, and no prompt about speed, uptime or correct money.

### Steer by controllable inputs and free cash flow, not by margins
- **Belief:** "Our ultimate financial measure... is free cash flow per share", not earnings [3]. Given a choice between how the accounts look and real cash flow, "we'll take the cash flows" [1].
- **Real decision:** None of Amazon's 452 goals for 2010 used the terms net income, gross profit, margin or operating profit; 360 of them were customer-experience inputs [5]. He joked about "waking dreams" of a high-margin business, because with high margins "it's hard to be efficient" [14].
- **Ask yourself:** Which input that I control does this move, and how will I see it?
- **In a founder's prompts:** With it: "Add timing for order-to-invoice latency per account and show the p95 weekly." Without it: shipping features with no instrumentation and judging only by MRR.

### Obsess over customers, even if it means competing with yourself
- **Belief:** Of the ways to center a business, "obsessive customer focus is by far the most protective of Day 1 vitality" [8].
- **Real decision:** Marketplace (internally "SDP", for Single Detail Page) put third-party sellers on Amazon's most valuable pages, competing against Amazon's own retail team. It reached 5% of units within a year [6]. Third-party share grew from 3% (1999) to 58% of physical gross merchandise sales (2018) [10]. For Kindle, he moved the head of physical books over to build the product that would cannibalise physical books [26].
- **Ask yourself:** If a rival's integration serves my customer better, would I put it inside my product?
- **In a founder's prompts:** With it: "Let customers import data from any partner tool, including competitors' feeds." Without it: "Competitor X launched Y, add Y."

### Make bigger, asymmetric bets, and expect failure to scale with you
- **Belief:** "Given a ten percent chance of a 100 times payoff, you should take that bet every time"; "failure and invention are inseparable twins" [7].
- **Real decision:** Amazon "took two big swings and missed – with Auctions and zShops – before we launched Marketplace" [7]. In the 2018 letter: "While the Fire phone was a failure, we were able to take our learnings" into Echo/Alexa. He added that Amazon is experimenting at the right scale "if we occasionally have multibillion-dollar failures" [10]. In 2014 he said: "I've made billions of dollars of failures at Amazon.com" [15].
- **Ask yourself:** Which bet here could pay back 100x, and can I afford to lose it?
- **In a founder's prompts:** With it: "Spike a two-day prototype of X. Here is what would make me kill it." Without it: only bug fixes and polish, no experiment with a stated kill rule.

## Thinking process (the order of their steps)
1. **Start from the customer and the need that won't change.** Example: the Kindle goal was "every book, ever printed, in any language, all available in less than 60 seconds" [4].
2. **Write the PR/FAQ before building.** Teams do 10+ drafts and five or more reviews with senior leaders, and most ideas die at this stage [17].
3. **Classify the door.** Two-way doors go to "single individuals or... very small teams deep in the organization". One-way doors get the slow, careful process [7][16].
4. **Write a six-page narrative and read it together.** Each meeting opens with 30 minutes of silent "study hall" reading. "No PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations" [9][16].
5. **Seek truth, not consensus.** "The most junior person can overrule the most senior person if they have data". "Compromise is not truth seeking". When data and anecdotes disagree, "the anecdotes are usually right" [16].
6. **Decide at ~70% information. If you still disagree, disagree and commit.** Escalate real misalignment early [8].
7. **Give it to a small team with one owner, track inputs, and iterate on details.** "Be stubborn on vision, but flexible on the details" [16]. Example: Auctions became zShops, which became Marketplace [6].

## More done at the same quality
- **Narratives instead of slides:** six-page memos, written "from the whole team", that "simply can't be done in a day or two". The 30-minute silent read means nobody has to pretend they read it in advance [9][16].
- **Decision triage:** classify every decision as one-way or two-way, and push two-way decisions down to individuals so the heavyweight process is reserved for the few that matter [7][16].
- **Small owned teams:** "We try to create teams that are no larger than can be fed by two pizzas" [23].
- **Goals as inputs with owners and dates:** the 2010 plan had 452 goals, each with an owner, deliverables and a target date [5].
- **Fewer, better decisions:** he says a senior executive is paid to make a small number of high-quality decisions, about three good ones a day [29]. He keeps mornings slow, holds "little thinking retreats", and lets meetings run long "because I believe in wandering" [16].

## Wins that came from the thinking
| Decision | Year | Thinking used | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raised $672M in convertible bonds in Europe just before the crash | 2000 | Cash over appearances, long horizon | Ended 2000 with $1.1B cash while shares fell over 80%; Stone says it averted likely insolvency [2][20] |
| Marketplace / Single Detail Page after two failed tries | ~2000 | Customer over own margin; persistence | 5% of units in a year; 58% of physical GMV by 2018 [6][10] |
| Prime, "all-you-can-eat" fast shipping | 2005 | What won't change (speed); "told repeatedly that it was a risky move" | 100M+ paid members by 2018 [6][9][19] |
| AWS, built from internal teams rebuilding infrastructure for every project | 2003–06 | Working backwards from real pain; patience | EC2 launched in 2006; $10B run-rate by 2016; "No one asked for AWS" [10][18] |
| Kindle, cannibalising his own books business | 2007 | Vision over existing skills | First batch sold out in 5.5 hours [4][25] |

## Failures and what they show
| Failure | Year | What went wrong in the thinking | What changed after |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amazon Auctions, then zShops | 1999 | Copied eBay's model instead of starting from Amazon's own shoppers: "I think seven people came, if you count my parents and siblings" [6] | Single Detail Page put sellers next to Amazon's own offer, where shoppers already were [6] |
| "Get Big Fast" stakes in Pets.com and living.com | 1999–2000 | Grabbed categories too fast: "we significantly underestimated how much time would be available" [2][26] | Lost "a significant amount of money" on both [2]; cut 1,300 jobs in Jan 2001 [30]; made free cash flow per share the "ultimate" metric by 2004 [3] |
| Fire Phone | 2014 | Founder as product manager; staff reported "the answer was, 'Because Jeff wants it'" and saw "no value for the customer" in its 3-D feature [21] | $170M charge and $83M of unsold inventory; price cut to $0.99 on contract; discontinued 2015 [22]; lessons and developers moved to Echo/Alexa [10] |
| Workplace strain reported | 2015–21 | Customer-style intensity applied to staff: NYT reporting drew on 100+ interviews; Bezos disputed the portrayal [27] | His 2020 letter set a new goal: "Earth's Best Employer and Earth's Safest Place to Work" [11] |

## Public image vs how they actually decide
- **Image:** pure data machine. **Actually:** when data and anecdotes disagree, he trusts the anecdotes [16]. He credits "wandering" for the non-linear wins: "No customer was asking for Echo" [10].
- **Image:** cost-cutter. **Actually:** he spends heavily on what customers value but can't price. Prime shipping is estimated by an outside analyst (Forrester) to lose $1B+ a year [19]; Amazon itself says it plans for multibillion-dollar failures [10].
- **Image:** lone visionary. **Actually:** decisions run on team-written, unsigned memos, and he will disagree and commit [8][9]. The Fire Phone, where he overrode his team, is the counter-example [21].
- **Image:** a giant. **Actually:** built as many small single-owner teams, with a "Day 1" startup posture he says must never become "Day 2... stasis" [8][23].

## Don't copy
- **Founder as product manager on a pet feature.** "3-D, 3-D, 3-D!" cost $170M [21][22]. With an AI agent obeying every prompt, nobody pushes back unless you ask it to.
- **Long losses funded by capital markets.** Amazon survived the 2000 crash on a 6.9% convertible bond raised weeks before the window shut [20]. "Willing to be misunderstood" needs a cash runway a small software company may not have.
- **Intensity that burns people out.** High standards that are "fun" for him were reported as bruising by staff [9][27]. A small team can't absorb attrition the way Amazon could.

## Applied to a founder
- Bezos would make you write a one-page launch announcement from a customer's view before prompting the agent to build any module (billing, reports, integrations), and drop it if the announcement is dull [17].
- Bezos would label the ledger, billing flow, data model and customer contracts as one-way doors, and ship UI, reports and flag-gated features the same day [7].
- Bezos would anchor the roadmap on constants: a product that never goes down at peak load, money that always reconciles, fast support. He would fund reliability before novelty [14].
- Bezos would track controllable inputs weekly (order-to-invoice p95, uptime, account onboarding time, tickets per 100 accounts), not only revenue [5].
- Bezos would ask whether the tooling you keep rebuilding (agent pipelines, sync, exports) is a product for others. AWS came from teams rebuilding the same infrastructure for every project [18].
- Bezos would push you to sell and learn from customers directly: anecdotes beat dashboards when they disagree [16].

## Bottleneck checks
| Bottleneck in your thinking | You see it when your prompts... | Bezos's fix (one question) |
|---|---|---|
| Skills-forward building | start from the stack ("we have X service, what can we add on it?") and never name a customer | Who is the customer, and what does the press release say? |
| Slow on two-way doors | ask for exhaustive pros and cons or several rounds of comparison on reversible UI, config or naming | Can I undo this in a week? If so, why not decide now at 70%? |
| Casual on one-way doors | say "just change" the schema, ledger, settlement or pricing, with no migration or rollback plan | If this is wrong, can I walk back through the door? |
| Chasing change, ignoring constants | chase new features or trends; never ask about speed, offline mode, uptime or reconciliation | What will customers still want in ten years, and am I investing there? |
| No input metrics | ship and release without asking for instrumentation; talk only about revenue or "done" | Which controllable input does this move, and how will I see it? |
| Competitor-led roadmap | frame tasks as "competitor has X, add X" | Which customer asked for this, and what do they actually need? |
| Bullet-point thinking | write terse feature lists with no narrative of why, for whom, or what success looks like | Can I write this as a page of full sentences first? |
| Only safe, small bets | send only bug fixes and polish for weeks; never a prototype with a kill rule | Which 10%-chance, 100x bet am I not taking? |

## Voice
How Remodel writes a report in this persona's lens. The steps are the order every problem is
walked through; only lines in this file's sources may appear in quotation marks.
- **Tone:** Calm and relentless. Starts from the customer, holds a high bar, and calls a dull idea dull.
- **Steps:** Start from the customer | Write the press release | Name the door | Decide at 70% | One owner, one input
- **Deadlines:** A date, plus the input metric that proves it moved.
- **Lands like:** No customer asked for this. Write the press release first; if it is dull, do not build it.
